10  Differential localisation analyses

10.1 From static maps to dynamic experiments

In the previous lessons we generated a static spatial map: for a single condition we used marker proteins and machine learning to predict where each protein resides in the cell. Whilst the steady-state localisation of proteins can provide vital information about function, these localisations are specific to the system of interest under the study conditions. Given the functional importance of protein localisation, the question naturally extends from profiling the spatial proteome of a single condition to comparing the spatial proteome between conditions. Correlation profiling methods have been widely applied to study protein dynamics in response to drugs, stress, signalling and differentiation (Itzhak et al. 2016; Orre et al. 2019; Mulvey et al. 2021; Christopher et al. 2025).

We say a protein is differentially localised (DL) if it is assigned to a different primary subcellular location (or niche) in one condition compared with another. Discovering these proteins is the main goal of a dynamic spatial proteomics experiment.

NoteWhat does “differentially localised” mean in practice?

A correlation profiling experiment measures the distribution of each protein across a set of biochemical fractions. If a protein relocates, its distribution across the fractions (its profile) changes shape so that it now looks like the profile of a different organelle. A change in the amount of a protein (expression) without a change in profile shape is not differential localisation. Such differences in abundance would not be discovered in a correlation profiling experiment since differences in abundance are removed during the row sum normalisation step.

We use the term differential localisation instead of translocation since changes in the distribution of a protein can be due to changes in localised protein turnover (synthesis and degradation) as well as active movement of proteins.

10.2 The use-case data: insulin-stimulated adipocytes

In this lesson we will use a dynamic LOPIT dataset of 3T3-L1 adipocytes (Conway et al. 2026). Cells were analysed under two conditions:

  1. basal (unstimulated)
  2. insulin-stimulated

Each condition was profiled in three biological replicates, and each replicate was fractionated into 10 fractions which were quantified with TMT 10-plex labelling. Replicate 1 of the basal condition was processed alongside replicate 1 of the insulin condition, and so on, which means the replicates are paired.

Insulin signalling in adipocytes is a classic example of regulated protein trafficking. The best-known example is the glucose transporter GLUT4 (Slc2a4), which is held in intracellular storage vesicles under basal conditions and is rapidly delivered to the plasma membrane in response to insulin, allowing the cell to take up glucose. We should therefore expect to see some proteins moving in this dataset.

10.3 BANDLE and SANDLE

In the workflow paper accompanying this course (Hutchings et al. 2025) we used BANDLE (Bayesian ANalysis of Differential Localisation Experiments) (Crook et al. 2022) to find differentially localised proteins. BANDLE is a fully Bayesian model, it uses Markov-chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to sample from the posterior distribution of its parameters, and it outputs a differential localisation probability for each protein. It is powerful, but it requires the user to set priors, run several long MCMC chains (typically on a high-performance computing cluster), and check that the chains have converged before the results can be used.

SANDLE (Statistical ANalysis of Differential Localisation Experiments) (Crook et al. 2026) was designed to answer the same question quickly and robustly, and to handle more complex experimental designs.

BANDLE SANDLE
Model Fully Bayesian (Gaussian processes + MCMC) Robust mixture model + regression test
Run time Minutes to hours; HPC often needed Seconds on a laptop
Priors to set Yes No
Convergence checks Yes No
Needs markers Yes Marker-based test yes, marker-free test no
Paired / multi-condition designs Supported (two conditions) Supported (two or more conditions)
Output DL probability + localisation predictions DL probability + p-value / FDR (need to merge with localisation predictions from elsewhere)


SANDLE combines two complementary tests:

  1. Marker-based test. Using the markers, SANDLE models each subcellular niche in each replicate with a robust multivariate t-distribution. The centre and spread of each niche are estimated with the minimum covariance determinant (MCD), which is insensitive to outlying markers, and graphical lasso regularisation keeps the estimates stable when a niche has few markers. Every protein then gets a probability of belonging to each niche, in each condition. The differential localisation (DL) probability compares these probabilities between the conditions: it is the probability that the niche a protein belongs to in the basal condition differs from the niche it belongs to in the insulin condition. In other words, it reflects our confidence that a protein changes compartmental identity (Crook et al. 2026). The test is called marker-based because it uses the markers to build its model of the niches.

  2. Marker-free test. This test ignores markers and organelles altogether. It asks whether the shape of a protein’s profile changes between the conditions by more than we would expect from replicate-to-replicate noise. The result is a classical p-value, corrected for multiple testing to give a false discovery rate (FDR). Whilst removing the requirement for marker proteins has benefits, a shift in correlation profile shape can only be interpreted as differential localisation when we don’t expect major changes in cell morphology. For example, if a stimulation results in nuclear swelling, the entire nucleus (and all proteins within this organelle) could have a differential profile.

The two tests capture different kinds of evidence: “is the protein assigned to a different organelle?” and “has its profile changed?”. Together they give each protein a DL probability and an FDR, which you can use to rank and explore candidates. There is no single correct way to decide which proteins are differentially localised, you might threshold on the DL probability, on the FDR, on both, or on either, and the thresholds themselves are a choice. Requiring both tests to pass gives a short, conservative list with few false positives (Crook et al. 2026), while thresholding on either test, or using looser thresholds, gives a longer list for exploration. The right choice depends on your biological question and on what you plan to do with the candidates next.

10.3.1 Our approach: localisation and movement as two separate steps

A dynamic experiment asks two different questions about each protein:

  1. Where is it? In which compartment does the protein reside in each condition?
  2. Does it move? Is there evidence that its localisation differs between the conditions?

We answer these separately. For the first, we use the localisations published in the original study of this dataset (Conway et al. 2026), which are provided with the data. For the second, we use the two SANDLE tests. We then combine the two: for each protein that SANDLE flags as a candidate mover, we look up where it is localised in each condition.

You could equally produce the localisations yourself with a classifier, such as the SVM we used in Part 2, applied to each condition in turn. Using the published localisations lets us focus this lesson on differential localisation, and lets us compare our results with the original study.

10.5 Importing the data

10.5.1 Why readMSnSet2()?

In the previous lessons we started from precursor-level output, processed it in a QFeatures object, aggregated it to protein-level and then coerced the protein-level sets into MSnSets with as(..., "MSnSet"), so that we could use pRoloc.

In practice, you may have been given data that has already been processed to protein level: a table exported from Proteome Discoverer or MaxQuant, the output of a collaborator’s pipeline, or a supplementary table from a paper. This data here was taken directly from supporting material of the Conway et al. (2026) paper. We can read the table straight into an MSnSet with the readMSnSet2() function from MSnbase. The end result is the same kind of object (an MSnSet) that we created by coercion at the start of part II, and everything we learnt about pRoloc applies to it.

NoteReminder: the three parts of an MSnSet

An MSnSet stores three linked tables:

  • exprs(): the quantitative data, with proteins in rows and samples (here, fractions) in columns
  • fData(): the feature data, with one row per protein and any information about it, such as marker annotation
  • pData(): the sample data, with one row per column of exprs(), such as the TMT tag, condition and replicate

readMSnSet2() builds the first two from a single spreadsheet. We add the third ourselves.

10.6 Exploring the data

Differential localisation analysis relies on the same thing as static localisation: well-resolved subcellular niches. Before we run any test we should therefore look at each replicate. Here we use plot2D() to plot the first two principal components (the default) of each replicate, coloured by marker.

## Set plot dimensions
par(mfrow = c(3, 3), mar = c(4, 4, 2, 1))

## Loop over reps 1-3 of basal
for (i in 1:3) {
  plot2D(control[[i]], fcol = "markers",
         main = paste("Basal, rep", i))
}

## Loop over reps 1-3 of insulin stimulated
for (i in 1:3) {
  plot2D(treated[[i]], fcol = "markers",
         main = paste("Insulin, rep", i))
}

addLegend(control[[1]], fcol = "markers", where = "other",
          ncol = 2, cex = 1.5)

We see clear, separated clusters for most organelles and a similar overall picture in every replicate of both conditions. Note that each PCA is computed separately, so the plots may be rotated or mirrored relative to each other. What matters is the relative position of the clusters.

AnswerAnswer

## Plot QSep results
par(mfrow = c(1, 2))

plot(qs_insulin, main = "Insulin, rep 1")

Overall resolution is similar in both conditions, which suggests that the data are of comparable quality i.e., resolution. This matters because if one condition were much less well resolved, we might see more apparent ‘movement’ simply because proteins are assigned less confidently. For example, if the ER and plasma membrane are well resolved from each other in one condition but not the other, there may be a large number of apparent movers between these compartments. However, resolution can also change for biological reasons. If treatment alters the size or density of an organelle, its sedimentation profile and separation from neighbouring compartments, will change. It is therefore worth comparing resolution per organelle as well as overall. If resolution for a specific organelle differs between conditions, this may reflect either technical variation (e.g. gradient quality) or a real change in that organelle. Comparing replicates within each condition and checking whether that organelle’s marker profiles shift between conditions can help distinguish these. Where resolution is reduced, protein assignments in that region are less certain, so apparent movements involving that organelle or its neighbours should be interpreted with caution.

NoteWhy not take the localisations from SANDLE?

The marker-based SANDLE test also calculates the probability that each protein belongs to each niche, and sandle_markers() reports the most probable niche in each condition. However, these probabilities are a step towards the DL probability, not a classification. There is no outlier or "unknown" class, so every protein is assigned to one of the marker niches however poorly it fits, and the model is not tuned or thresholded in the way a classifier is. We therefore take localisations from a dedicated classification, and use SANDLE for differential localisation.

10.8 Step 2: Which proteins move?

We now run the two SANDLE tests on the per-replicate data. Neither test uses the final localisations: they give each protein a score for whether it moves, which we will combine with the predicted localisations in Step 3 below.

10.8.1 Marker-based SANDLE

The marker-based test is a single function call. sandle_markers() fits a robust t-distribution to every niche in every replicate, computes the niche probabilities for every protein, and combines them into a DL probability. We pass the two lists of replicates and the name of the marker column.

The robust fitting step uses random subsampling, so we set a seed to make the result reproducible.

set.seed(1)

The output is a data.frame with one row per protein. The columns we use are:

  • dl_prob: the differential localisation probability
  • is_marker: whether the protein is a marker. Markers are given a DL probability of 0 because they are assumed not to move

The remaining columns give the most probable niche in each condition and its probability. As explained above, we do not use these as localisations.

Since the results of marker-based SANDLE are intrinsically related to the quality of the markers provided, it is good practice to visualise the markers that we used as input data. We look at the fitted profile (mean ± 1 standard deviation) of each niche in one replicate. Each organelle should have a distinct and reasonably tight profile.

## QC check - plot fitted profile of markers for each niche
plot_marker_profiles(marker_res, replicate = 1)

These marker profiles look good. In practice, we would recommend doing this for each replicate of each condition.

NoteHow the DL probability is computed

For each protein, SANDLE averages the niche probabilities over the replicates of each condition. The DL probability is then the probability that a niche drawn for the basal condition differs from a niche drawn for the insulin condition, \(1 - \sum_k p_k^{\text{basal}}\, p_k^{\text{insulin}}\). A protein confidently assigned to the same niche in both conditions scores close to 0; a protein confidently assigned to different niches scores close to 1.

Because the DL probability uses the full set of niche probabilities, and not just the single most probable niche, it responds to any change in how a protein’s probability is spread across the niches. This includes a protein that leaves an organelle and ends up between several, or one that becomes partly associated with a second niche. What it does not see are changes in profile that leave the niche probabilities unchanged, for example a shift within a single niche. Note also that a protein that is uncertain in both conditions gets a non-zero DL probability even if it does not move, because two uncertain niche draws can differ by chance.

NoteRecap: what each measure tells us
Measure Column Question it answers Comes from
DL probability dl_prob How confident are we that the protein changes compartment? Marker-based test
FDR fdr Is the change in profile bigger than replicate-to-replicate noise? Marker-free test
Effect size RSS_diff How large is the change in profile? Marker-free test
Localisation loc_basal, loc_insulin Where is the protein in each condition? Published classification (Step 1)
  • A high DL probability means the protein’s niche probabilities differ between conditions, so it has probably changed compartment. It says nothing about how large the change in profile is.
  • A low FDR means the profile change is consistent across replicates and unlikely to be noise. A very small but reproducible change can still have a low FDR.
  • A large effect size means a large share of the protein has shifted to different fractions. On its own it says nothing about noise, so a large but inconsistent change can have a large effect size and a high FDR. We use it to rank candidates and to prioritise them for follow-up.
  • The localisations tell us where a protein moves from and to. They do not tell us whether it moves: that is what the DL probability and FDR are for.

A convincing mover typically has a high DL probability, a low FDR and a reasonably large effect size. In a differential expression analysis, the FDR and effect size play the same roles as the adjusted p-value and fold change.

Each type of movement tells us something different:

  • compartment → compartment: the protein is confidently classified to one compartment in each condition, and they differ. These are the clearest candidates.
  • compartment → unknown and unknown → compartment: the protein leaves, or arrives at, a confidently classified compartment, but its other location is uncertain. It may move to a compartment that is not represented in the markers, become distributed across several compartments, or sit between clusters.
  • unknown → unknown: the profile changes, but the protein cannot be confidently classified in either condition. These are often proteins that sit between compartments or are in more than one location (i.e., multi-localised).
  • same compartment: the profile changes, but the protein is classified to the same compartment in both conditions. These may reflect partial relocation, where only a fraction of the protein moves, a shift within a compartment, or a change that the markers cannot resolve. Crook et al. (2026) interpret proteins with strong marker-free support but a low DL probability in the same way, as “more complex or heterogeneous spatial reorganisation rather than simple compartmental shifts”.

10.11 Interpreting the results

Computational prediction of differential localisation is largely exploratory. LOPIT data contains noise between fractions and replicates, and some proteins naturally sit between organelles or in several at once. The results should be used as a starting point for further investigation (for example, searching the literature for evidence of movement, imaging, or biochemical follow-up), rather than as validation in themselves.

Some things to keep in mind:

  • Localisation and movement are separate questions. A classification tells us where a protein is in each condition; SANDLE tells us whether it moves. A protein can move without changing its localisation, and a change in localisation near a threshold is not, on its own, evidence of movement.
  • Markers are assumed not to move. If a marker protein does relocate, the marker-based test cannot detect it, and it may distort both the classification and the niche model. The marker-free test does not have this limitation, but it also flags consistent shifts of whole compartments between conditions, as we saw in Challenge 4.
  • Many movers are "unknown". Proteins that move often do not sit neatly within one compartment. Movements to and from "unknown" are real results, not failures, but they need careful checking of the profiles.
  • Results depend on data quality. Poorly resolved compartments lead to uncertain assignments, which in turn inflate DL probabilities. Always check resolution first.
  • The marker-based test has a random component. The robust fitting step uses random subsampling, particularly for niches with few markers. Set a seed for reproducibility, and treat proteins close to a threshold with caution.
  • Thresholds are your choice. This applies to the localisations, the DL probability and the FDR. Requiring both a high DL probability and a low FDR gives a short list supported by two kinds of evidence. Using either test, or looser thresholds, gives a longer list for exploration. Choose based on your question and how you will follow up the candidates.
TipKey Points
  • A protein is differentially localised if it changes subcellular niche between conditions, seen as a change in the shape of its profile across fractions.
  • We answer two separate questions: where each protein is in each condition, from a classification such as the published localisations or an SVM, and whether it moves, using SANDLE.
  • SANDLE detects differential localisation in seconds, with no priors, MCMC or HPC, making it a fast and robust alternative to BANDLE.
  • The marker-based test models each niche with a robust t-distribution and gives a DL probability. The marker-free test compares profiles between conditions and gives a p-value and FDR without needing any markers.
  • Combining the SANDLE scores with the localisations shows where the movers go. Many move to or from "unknown", reflecting proteins that sit between compartments or in more than one location.
  • How to threshold, and on which test, is up to you: stringent for a short list, looser for exploration. Candidates should always be checked by plotting profiles and spatial maps, and followed up experimentally.
